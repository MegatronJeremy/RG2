\section{Summary against the assignment's goals}

The assignment's goal, four ray-traced lighting effects (shadows, ambient occlusion, reflections,
and diffuse global illumination) through inline ray queries in a custom Vulkan engine, characterized
for cost and quality, is met. All four share one hit-shading routine over a bindless geometry table
and one reusable SVGF denoiser, and sit inside an existing rasterizing forward renderer rather than a
standalone tracer (Chapter~\ref{ch:implementacija}). Table~\ref{tab:summary} collects the headline
numbers from Chapter~\ref{ch:analiza}. The two cards do not agree on which effect costs most: on the
RX 9070 XT shadows dominate, while on the RTX 5070 shadows, reflections and global illumination sit
within \perfNVSpread{} ms of one another, inside the run-to-run spread, so on that card they are
indistinguishable in cost.

The ray-count knee sits at one ray per pixel: going from one to eight rays costs \rcGpuPct\% more GPU
time for a \rcPsnrGain{} dB PSNR gain, because the denoiser and temporal accumulation already recover
most of the achievable quality from a single sample (Section~\ref{sec:raycount}). The spatial
denoiser's own contribution is likewise not where a static metric can see it: it moves PSNR, SSIM and
FLIP by less than run-to-run noise, while cutting frame-to-frame RMS on a static view from
\flickerOff{} to \flickerOn{}, a \flickerPct\% reduction, for \perfDenoiseAMD{} ms
(Section~\ref{sec:denoise-eval}). At this sample count the temporal history does the reconstruction
and the spatial filter buys stability rather than static fidelity.

The practical value is a hybrid ray-query pipeline that gives correct off-screen lighting at an
interactive budget without a full path tracer.

\section{Limitations}

The measurements are taken on two GPUs of comparable market tier and a single scene class, so the
absolute numbers do not generalize across hardware or content. Global illumination is limited to a
single diffuse bounce, and shadows are hard or lightly softened rather than physically accurate area
shadows. To stay within a real-time budget the ray counts are kept very low (one or two per pixel),
which places heavy demand on the denoiser and exposes its failure modes: temporal ghosting and lag
under fast motion, particularly for reflections, which reuse the diffuse-tuned temporal reprojection.
The top-level acceleration structure is fully rebuilt on every dynamic frame rather than refit, which
is heavier than necessary for small transform changes. Inline ray queries give up the hardware ray
scheduling and recursion a ray-tracing pipeline would provide, a deliberate tradeoff for integration
simplicity that would matter for deeper or more numerous bounces.

\section{Future work}

Sampling is the most immediate direction: replacing the naive per-light and cosine-hemisphere
sampling with reservoir resampling (ReSTIR and ReSTIR GI) would improve quality at equal ray count,
especially for many lights and for indirect paths \cite{bitterli2020restir, ouyang2021restirgi}. On
reconstruction, the engine already contains a temporal neural network used as an anti-aliasing
resolve, so a learned denoiser or upscaler replacing or augmenting SVGF is a natural next step,
together with the vendor-neutral cooperative-matrix inference study that motivated an earlier version
of this project. Denoised shadows could scale to many lights more cheaply with a MegaLights-style
stochastic per-pixel light selection on the shared denoiser, rather than tracing every light every
frame. A dedicated, penumbra-aware shadow denoiser in place of the shared one is a related
refinement. Broadening the cross-vendor comparison beyond two current-generation cards, plus a second
rendering backend (DirectX 12), would extend the performance story further.
