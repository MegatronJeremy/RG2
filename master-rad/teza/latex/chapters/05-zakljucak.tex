\section{Summary against the assignment's goals}

The assignment's goal, four ray-traced lighting effects (shadows, ambient occlusion, reflections,
and diffuse global illumination) through inline ray queries in a custom Vulkan engine, characterized
for cost and quality, is met. All four share one hit-shading routine over a bindless geometry table
and one reusable SVGF denoiser, and sit inside an existing rasterizing forward renderer rather than a
standalone tracer (Chapter~\ref{ch:implementacija}). Table~\ref{tab:summary} collects the headline
numbers from Chapter~\ref{ch:analiza}. The two cards do not agree on which effect costs most: the
most expensive is \perfTopEffectAMD{} on the RX 9070 XT and \perfTopEffectNV{} on the RTX 5070, and
each winner is separated from every other effect by more than the run-to-run spread, so the
disagreement is a result rather than a tie being broken by noise. It reaches the engineering decision
itself: with this scene's five lights the stochastic shadow strategy is the cheaper of the two on the
RX 9070 XT (\perfShadowStochAMD{} against \perfShadowInlineAMD{} ms) and \perfShadowRatioNV{}x the
more expensive on the RTX 5070 (\perfShadowStochNV{} against \perfShadowInlineNV{} ms), so a renderer
that hard-codes either is wrong on one of these two cards
(Section~\ref{sec:analiza-shadow-strategy}).

The ray-count knee sits at one ray per pixel: going from one to eight rays costs \rcGpuPct\% more GPU
time for a \rcPsnrGain{} dB PSNR gain, inside the run-to-run spread of the metric, because the
denoiser and temporal accumulation already recover most of the achievable quality from a single
sample (Section~\ref{sec:raycount}). Spatial sample density behaves the same way: tracing GI and AO
at full rather than half resolution costs \hrDeltaMs{} ms, a \hrDeltaPct\% increase on an
\hrHalfTotal{} ms frame, and returns \hrPsnrGain{} dB (Section~\ref{sec:halfres}). The spatial
denoiser's own contribution is likewise not where a static metric can see it: it moves PSNR, SSIM and
FLIP by less than run-to-run noise, while cutting frame-to-frame RMS on a static view from
\flickerOff{} to \flickerOn{}, a \flickerPct\% reduction, for \perfDenoiseAMD{} ms
(Section~\ref{sec:denoise-eval}). At this sample count the temporal history does the reconstruction
and the spatial filter buys stability rather than static fidelity. The cost of inlining a ray query
is also visible statically: on the RX 9070 XT the forward shading pass rises from 87 to 130 registers
per thread with the inline shadow query, crossing an allocation boundary that drops it from 16 to 10
concurrently resident waves, and it is the only occupancy-limited shader on the render path on either
card (Section~\ref{sec:occupancy}).

The practical value is a hybrid ray-query pipeline that resolves lighting from geometry outside the
camera's view, which no screen-space method can, for \perfFourEffectsAMD{} ms of the frame on the
RX 9070 XT and \perfFourEffectsNV{} ms on the RTX 5070, without a full path tracer.

\section{Limitations}

The measurements are taken on two GPUs of comparable market tier and a single scene class, so the
absolute numbers do not generalize across hardware or content. Content coverage is the weakest part
of the evaluation by a published standard: ITU-T P.910 \cite{itu_p910} asks for at least four source
sequences spanning a range of spatial and temporal complexity, against the one scene and two routes
used here, so an artifact specific to other content would not be found by this protocol. Global illumination is limited to a
single diffuse bounce, and shadows are hard or lightly softened rather than physically accurate area
shadows. To stay within a real-time budget the ray counts are kept very low (one or two per pixel per
effect, and two per light for inline shadows),
which places heavy demand on the denoiser and exposes its failure modes: temporal ghosting and lag
under fast motion, particularly for reflections, which reuse the diffuse-tuned temporal reprojection.
The top-level acceleration structure is fully rebuilt on every dynamic frame rather than refit, which
is heavier than necessary for small transform changes. Inline ray queries give up the hardware ray
scheduling and recursion a ray-tracing pipeline would provide, a deliberate tradeoff for integration
simplicity that would matter for deeper or more numerous bounces.

\section{Future work}

Sampling is the most immediate direction. The stochastic shadow path already resamples within a
frame, so the missing piece is reservoir reuse across neighbouring pixels and across frames (ReSTIR,
and ReSTIR GI for the indirect paths), which is what buys quality at equal ray count when the light
count is high \cite{bitterli2020restir, ouyang2021restirgi}. The cosine-weighted hemisphere sampling
behind GI and AO is untouched by that work and remains naive. On reconstruction, a learned denoiser
or upscaler replacing or augmenting SVGF is a natural next step. The stochastic shadow strategy
itself is a prototype rather than a finished technique: making it the default would first require
removing the artifacts it shows under camera motion where two point lights overlap. A dedicated,
penumbra-aware shadow denoiser in place of the shared one is a related refinement. Broadening the cross-vendor comparison beyond two current-generation cards, plus a second
rendering backend (DirectX 12), would extend the performance story further.
