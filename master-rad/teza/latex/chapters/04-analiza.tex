\chapter{Analiza}
\label{ch:analiza}

This chapter evaluates the system. It first fixes the methodology, hardware, scene, and the two
measurement setups (Section~\ref{sec:metodologija}), then reports the per-effect GPU cost
(Section~\ref{sec:cena}), the ray-count versus quality and speed tradeoff
(Section~\ref{sec:raycount}), the contribution of the denoiser (Section~\ref{sec:denoise-eval}), a
comparison against the existing solutions from Chapter~\ref{ch:pregled} (Section~\ref{sec:poredjenje}),
and the static shader occupancy (Section~\ref{sec:occupancy}). Every number reported here is produced
by a measured run; cells still marked as pending have not yet been measured and are not estimated.

\section{Metodologija}
\label{sec:metodologija}

\paragraph{Hardver: cross-vendor postavka.} The central evaluation compares the identical pipeline on
two current-generation GPUs from different vendors: an AMD Radeon RX 9070 XT (RDNA4) and an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 5070 (Blackwell). Both run the same engine binary, the same HLSL-compiled SPIR-V shaders,
the same render graph, and the same scene and settings; the only variable is the selected physical
device. That identity is the fairness basis of the comparison: any difference in timing is due to the
hardware and its driver, not to a different code path. Table~\ref{tab:gpus} lists the relevant
specifications. Because the algorithm is bit-for-bit identical on both GPUs, the quality metrics
(PSNR/SSIM) are expected to match across them; the cross-vendor result is therefore a performance
comparison, and any quality divergence would flag a non-determinism to investigate rather than a real
difference. The static occupancy analysis (Section~\ref{sec:occupancy}) targets a separate AMD
architecture (RDNA3) offline and is independent of this cross-vendor comparison.

\paragraph{OMM support disparity.} The ``identical code path'' claim above has one exception. Sponza
carries three MASK-mode (alpha-cutout) materials; a cutout mesh traces through the opacity-micromap
BLAS on a device exposing \texttt{VK\_EXT\_opacity\_micromap} and through the any-hit alpha-test
fallback otherwise (Section~\ref{sec:accel}). On the test machines the NVIDIA RTX 5070 driver exposes
the extension and the AMD RX 9070 XT driver does not, despite comparable RDNA4/Blackwell hardware
capability, so the two vendors resolve masked geometry through genuinely different acceleration-structure
code for this scene. The disparity only changes how a masked microtriangle is classified during
traversal (skipping the any-hit shader when OMM marks it opaque or transparent, running it when
unknown); it does not change ray direction, hit position, or shading, so it cannot explain any
quality-metric difference. It is a small, one-directional, driver-dependent contributor to the
per-effect GPU-ms gap wherever a shadow, AO, reflection, or GI ray actually hits cutout geometry in a
given viewpoint, and this evaluation does not isolate it further.

\paragraph{Scena.} The test scene is Sponza, a standard interior with strong indirect bounce, lit by
ten lights (three spot, six point, one directional) at a fixed render resolution stated with the
results. A code-generated stress scene (a grid of meshes) is available for scaling tests.

\paragraph{Controls for fair comparison.} Several controls keep the measurements honest and
reproducible. The camera is held static for every A/B comparison: a moving camera desynchronizes the
frame sequence between two runs and fabricates differences that are timing artifacts rather than real
effects. The shader cache is cleared before any GPU metric is trusted, since a stale cache produces
nondeterministic results. Performance baselines are per-machine and per-resolution, because absolute
milliseconds are not comparable across GPUs or output sizes. Persisted console variables are ignored
during a benchmark run so that a leftover setting cannot silently change the configuration.

\paragraph{Performance measurement.} Each render-graph pass is wrapped in a GPU timestamp scope, and
a harness (\texttt{perf-bench}) averages those per-pass timings over a fixed frame budget after a
fifteen-frame warmup that also covers the one-frame timestamp latency. The benchmark sweeps a fixed
matrix of configurations, \texttt{rt-off} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{shadows} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{+ao}
$\rightarrow$ \texttt{+refl} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{+gi}, enabling one more effect at each step, so the
cost of an effect is the difference between adjacent configurations. That difference is always taken
on \emph{total} GPU time, not a single pass scope: shadows and reflections are inline within the
Forward pass, so isolating their cost needs the whole-frame total rather than a per-pass number, and
ambient occlusion and global illumination are reported the same way for consistency across the table,
even though they do run as their own half-resolution passes with separate timestamp scopes. A
trailing \texttt{ssgi} configuration swaps the ray-traced GI pass for the screen-space producer on top
of \texttt{+refl}, so it is diffed against \texttt{+refl} rather than against \texttt{+gi}: the two
are alternatives, not cumulative. Across vendors, the raw timestamps are converted to milliseconds
using each device's timestamp period, so the per-pass times are directly comparable; results are
reported for both GPUs side by side rather than diffed against a single-machine baseline, since here
the cross-vendor comparison is itself the measurement.

\paragraph{Quality measurement.} Reconstruction quality is measured against a converged
\emph{path-traced} reference (Section~\ref{sec:pathtracer}): a brute-force unidirectional path tracer
with next-event estimation, accumulated over many frames on a static view, which is the correctness
anchor for every effect at once (shadows, ambient occlusion, reflections, and indirect). For each
real-time technique the harness (\texttt{quality-bench}) captures the same tonemapped present as the
reference and reports three image-quality metrics against it: \textbf{FLIP} (a perceptual difference
metric), \textbf{PSNR}, and \textbf{SSIM}. The metrics are averaged over several fixed viewpoints
(atrium, floor, and gallery views of Sponza) so a single view cannot bias the result, which is how
real-time global-illumination and denoiser work is evaluated in practice. Every quality figure in
this chapter is captured on a single device, the RX 9070 XT; because the algorithm is bit-for-bit
identical across vendors (see above), the resulting metrics are not expected to depend on which GPU
produced them.

On both cards reflections, not shadows, are the single most expensive effect (RX 9070 XT 2.80 ms
versus 2.34 ms for shadows; RTX 5070 2.52 ms versus 1.23 ms), ahead of global illumination and

ray-traced GI at the same point in the ladder (9070 XT 1.01 ms versus 1.73 ms; RTX 5070 0.79 ms
versus 1.25 ms), the expected screen-space-versus-ray-traced cost tradeoff; Section~\ref{sec:poredjenje}

\section{Broj zrakova naspram kvaliteta i brzine}
\label{sec:raycount}

This section sweeps the GI ray count per pixel through the full pipeline (denoiser and temporal
resolve enabled, settled over 90 frames, RX 9070 XT), plotting reconstruction quality against the
path-traced reference and GPU time against the ray count, to find the knee past which additional
rays stop paying for themselves.

\begin{table}[htb]
\centering
\caption{GI kvalitet i GPU cena u funkciji broja zraka po pikselu (denoiser i temporalni resolve
uklju\v{c}eni, RX 9070 XT).}
\label{tab:raycount}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrr@{}}
\toprule
Zraka/piksel & PSNR (dB) & SSIM & FLIP & GPU ukupno (ms) \\
\midrule
1 & 24.53 & 0.7358 & 0.1487 & 3.53 \\
2 & 24.65 & 0.7394 & 0.1452 & 3.89 \\
4 & 24.67 & 0.7404 & 0.1462 & 4.21 \\
8 & 24.73 & 0.7429 & 0.1437 & 6.29 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The knee sits at 1 ray per pixel. Going from 1 to 8 rays costs 78\% more GPU time (3.53 to 6.29 ms)
for a 0.20 dB PSNR gain and a FLIP improvement from 0.1487 to 0.1437, both within run-to-run noise on
this metric. The denoiser and temporal accumulation already recover most of the achievable quality
from a single ray; once they are in the pipeline, additional samples per pixel are not where this
engine's ray budget should go. This is consistent with the SVGF design goal (spatiotemporal
filtering to make 1 sample per pixel viable) rather than a property specific to this
implementation. Figure~\ref{fig:spp-sweep} shows the raw noise this filtering is masking, at the two
extremes of the sweep. The capture disables the denoiser, the temporal resolve, and TAA, and is cut
off at a fixed 30-frame cap. Disabling TAA is what makes the figure honest: TAA is itself a temporal
accumulator, so with it enabled the capture settles to the same high-frequency energy as the denoised
image (5.9 in both cases) and the ray count appears to make no difference. With it off the ray count
separates cleanly, from 10.8 at one ray per pixel to 7.3 at eight.

\label{sec:denoise-eval}

With temporal accumulation already active, the spatial SVGF pass changes reconstruction quality
against the static-frame metrics (PSNR, SSIM, FLIP) by less than measurement noise: the temporal
history is doing essentially all of the visible convergence at this settled frame, and the spatial
filter's own contribution shows up elsewhere, in temporal stability rather than static quality. A
frame-to-frame RMS delta on the same static view (frame $N$ against $N{+}1$ at steady state, a
flicker proxy under a static camera, not motion) drops from 0.3229 with the denoiser off to 0.1949
with it on, a 40\% reduction: the spatial filter suppresses the per-frame noise that temporal
accumulation alone leaves as flicker. The GPU cost of the denoiser pass itself is small, 3.739 to
3.810 ms total (a $\sim$0.07 ms delta) against the same GI-enabled baseline used for the raycount
sweep in Section~\ref{sec:raycount}; the register-limited \texttt{GIDenoise.comp} shader from
Section~\ref{sec:denoising} is inexpensive in isolation; its cost is amortized further because it
runs at half resolution.

\section{Pore\dj enje sa postoje\'cim re\v{s}enjima}
\label{sec:poredjenje}

This section compares the ray-traced effects against the existing solutions surveyed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:pregled}. The engine implements raster screen-space baselines for ambient occlusion and
reflections (SSAO, SSR; Section~\ref{sec:efekti}), so the comparison is a same-scene, same-camera A/B: the
screen-space version against the ray-traced version, showing the cases the screen-space method
structurally cannot handle (off-screen occluders and reflectors, disocclusion) and the cost
difference (Figure~\ref{fig:rt-vs-ss}). Separately, the real-time hybrid is compared against the
path-traced reference of Section~\ref{sec:metodologija} in FLIP, PSNR, and SSIM, to show how close it comes and
where it diverges. The positioning against production systems (Lumen, RTXGI, ReSTIR) is qualitative:
the contribution is an integrated, measured, openly licensed ray-query implementation, not a system
that beats a production global-illumination solution.

Table~\ref{tab:quality} reports each technique's image quality against the path-traced reference,
averaged over the three viewpoints.

Two results stand out. First, global illumination is the dominant quality lever: RT GI alone lifts
PSNR from 15.2 (raster) to 25.2 and cuts FLIP by more than two thirds, and enabling all effects
reaches FLIP 0.087, PSNR 30.7, SSIM 0.87, by far the closest to the reference. Second, at these
whole-frame metrics screen-space and ray-traced AO and reflections are near-parity (SSAO versus RT AO,
and SSR versus RT reflections, differ by under 0.01 in FLIP), because the occlusion and reflection
contributions are small at the whole-frame level; the ray-traced advantage is off-screen correctness,
which a whole-frame average does not isolate and which the per-view captures (Figure~\ref{fig:rt-vs-ss})
make visible. Screen-space GI sits between those two tiers: its FLIP (0.341) is markedly better than
the AO/reflection pairs but well short of RT GI (0.141), consistent with SSGI only gathering bounce
light from on-screen geometry, where RT GI also reaches off-screen and behind-camera surfaces.
