\section{Problem i motivacija}

Real-time rendering is dominated by rasterization, which resolves primary visibility (what is
directly seen from the camera) very efficiently but has no native notion of what any surface point
can see beyond itself. The effects that make an image look real depend on exactly that: indirect
illumination bouncing between surfaces, contact shadows, glossy reflections, and ambient occlusion
all answer questions of the form ``what else is visible from here.'' Real-time engines have long
approximated them.
Screen-space methods (SSAO, screen-space reflections and global illumination) are cheap but see only
what is currently on screen, so they fail wherever the needed geometry is off-screen or newly
disoccluded \cite{mittring2007cryengine, bavoil2008hbao}. Precomputed methods bake lighting into
lightmaps or probes and cannot follow dynamic geometry. Voxel approaches such as voxel cone tracing
are scene-wide but coarse and prone to light leaking \cite{crassin2011vxgi}.

Hardware ray tracing changes what is affordable. Recent GPUs (NVIDIA RTX from 2018, AMD RDNA2 from
2020) added fixed-function units for bounding-volume-hierarchy traversal and ray-triangle
intersection, so casting a ray against the whole scene became fast enough to do per pixel at
interactive rates. This reopens the effects above as first-class, physically grounded passes that
sample the true scene rather than a screen-space proxy. The catch is that a full solution of the
rendering equation \cite{kajiya1986rendering} by path tracing remains far too expensive for
real-time at display resolution on this hardware, and any affordable ray budget (one or a few rays
per pixel) produces heavy noise. The practical path, and the one this thesis takes, is therefore
hybrid: rasterize primary visibility, add the secondary effects as ray-traced passes at low sample
counts, and reconstruct a clean image with a denoiser \cite{schied2017svgf}
(Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).

\section{Za\v{s}to hibridni pristup i za\v{s}to ray query (a ne RT-pipeline)}

Vulkan offers two ways to trace rays, a dedicated ray-tracing pipeline and inline ray queries
(\texttt{VK_KHR_ray_query}); Section~\ref{sec:vulkanrt} sets them side by side.

This work uses inline ray queries for architectural fit: the engine is a rasterizing forward
renderer, and inline queries let each effect drop in as an ordinary compute or fragment pass
without restructuring the renderer around a ray-tracing pipeline and its binding table. Effects can
be added one at a time, which matches a hybrid design where rays are a targeted addition on top of
rasterization rather than the primary architecture. The price is giving up hardware ray scheduling
across shader stages and recursion, although the effects here are shallow (a single bounce) and
short, so that price is small.

\section{Cilj i istra\v{z}iva\v{c}ko pitanje}

The goal is to implement four ray-traced lighting effects, shadows, ambient occlusion, reflections,
and diffuse global illumination, through inline ray queries in a custom Vulkan engine, and to
characterize each one. The research question breaks into measurable sub-questions: the per-effect
GPU cost in milliseconds; the effect of samples per pixel on quality (PSNR/SSIM) and on time; the
quality and cost of the denoiser with it enabled versus disabled; the impact of tracing at half
versus full resolution; and how the identical Vulkan implementation performs across GPU vendors
(AMD RDNA4 versus NVIDIA Blackwell). The scope is deliberately bounded: a single scene class, two
current-generation GPUs of comparable tier (Table~\ref{tab:gpus}), diffuse global illumination, and a
converged path-traced reference for the quality metrics.

\section{Doprinosi}

The contributions are: an integrated, openly licensed implementation of four hybrid ray-query
lighting effects inside one custom Vulkan engine; a per-effect GPU cost breakdown produced by a
reproducible A/B benchmark harness rather than ad-hoc captures; an evaluation of a self-implemented
SVGF denoiser shared across all four signals, covering the quality it recovers, its cost, and its
temporal stability; a reusable half-resolution, G-buffer-prepass, denoise-and-upsample pattern; and
a rarely reported same-code performance comparison of the identical ray-query pipeline on AMD RDNA4
and NVIDIA Blackwell hardware.

\section{Struktura rada}
