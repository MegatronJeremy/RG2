\section{Problem and motivation}

Real-time rendering is dominated by rasterization, which resolves primary visibility (what is
directly seen from the camera) very efficiently but has no native notion of what any surface point
can see beyond itself. The effects that make an image look real depend on exactly that: indirect
illumination bouncing between surfaces, contact shadows, glossy reflections, and ambient occlusion
all answer questions of the form ``what else is visible from here.'' Real-time engines have long
approximated them.
Screen-space methods (SSAO, screen-space reflections and global illumination) are cheap but see only
what is currently on screen, so they fail wherever the needed geometry is off-screen or newly
disoccluded \cite{mittring2007cryengine, bavoil2008hbao}. Precomputed methods bake lighting into
lightmaps or probes and cannot follow dynamic geometry. Voxel approaches such as voxel cone tracing
are scene-wide but coarse and prone to light leaking \cite{crassin2011vxgi}.

Hardware ray tracing changes what is affordable. Recent GPUs (NVIDIA RTX from 2018, AMD RDNA2 from
2020) added fixed-function units for bounding-volume-hierarchy traversal and ray-triangle
intersection, so casting a ray against the whole scene became fast enough to do per pixel at
interactive rates. This reopens the effects above as first-class, physically grounded passes that
sample the true scene rather than a screen-space proxy. The catch is that a full solution of the
rendering equation \cite{kajiya1986rendering}, which states how much light leaves a surface point in
a given direction as emission plus an integral over all incoming directions, by path tracing remains
far too expensive for real-time at display resolution on this hardware, and any affordable ray budget
(one or a few rays per pixel) samples only a handful of the many light paths reaching each pixel, so
the estimate is right on average but wrong per pixel, which shows up as noise. The practical path, and the one this thesis takes, is therefore
hybrid: rasterize primary visibility, add the secondary effects as ray-traced passes at low sample
counts, and reconstruct a clean image with a denoiser \cite{schied2017svgf}
(Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).

\section{Why a hybrid approach and why ray query (not the RT pipeline)}

Vulkan offers two ways to trace rays, a dedicated ray-tracing pipeline and inline ray queries
(\texttt{VK_KHR_ray_query}). Section~\ref{sec:vulkanrt} sets them side by side.

This work uses inline ray queries for architectural fit: the engine is a rasterizing forward
renderer, and inline queries let each effect drop in as an ordinary compute or fragment pass
without restructuring the renderer around a ray-tracing pipeline and its binding table. Effects can
be added one at a time, which matches a hybrid design where rays are a targeted addition on top of
rasterization rather than the primary architecture. The price is that the implementation can no longer
reorder ray work across shader invocations to keep divergent paths coherent, and that recursion must
be written as an explicit loop rather than a shader spawning a new ray, although the effects here are
shallow (a single bounce) and short, so that price is small.

\section{Goal and research question}

The goal is to implement four ray-traced lighting effects, shadows, ambient occlusion, reflections,
and diffuse global illumination, through inline ray queries in a custom Vulkan engine, and to
characterize each one. The research question breaks into measurable sub-questions: the per-effect
GPU cost in milliseconds; the effect of samples per pixel on quality (PSNR/SSIM) and on time; the
quality and cost of the denoiser with it enabled versus disabled; the impact of tracing at half
versus full resolution; and how the identical Vulkan implementation performs across GPU vendors
(AMD RDNA4 versus NVIDIA Blackwell). The scope is deliberately bounded: a single indoor scene
(Sponza), two
current-generation GPUs of comparable tier (Table~\ref{tab:gpus}), diffuse global illumination, and a
converged path-traced reference for the quality metrics.

Three of the answers run against intuition. Eight times the ray budget buys a PSNR gain that sits
inside run-to-run noise, so the curve is already flat at one ray per pixel
(Section~\ref{sec:raycount}). The denoiser earns its cost in temporal stability rather than in static
fidelity (Section~\ref{sec:motion}). And a single shader crossing a 96-register allocation boundary
falls from 16 concurrently resident waves to 10, which is where the inline shadow query's cost
becomes visible statically (Section~\ref{sec:occupancy}).

\section{Contributions}

The contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item an integrated, openly licensed implementation of four hybrid ray-query lighting effects inside
one custom Vulkan engine;
\item a per-effect GPU cost breakdown produced by a reproducible A/B benchmark harness rather than
ad-hoc captures;
\item a reusable half-resolution, G-buffer-prepass, denoise-and-upsample pattern;
\item an evaluation of a self-implemented SVGF denoiser shared across all four signals, covering the
quality it recovers, its cost, and its temporal stability;
\item a same-code performance comparison of the identical ray-query pipeline on AMD RDNA4 and NVIDIA
Blackwell hardware, which shows the ranking of the two shadow strategies inverting between vendors
(Section~\ref{sec:analiza-shadow-strategy}, Table~\ref{tab:shadow-strategy}): stochastic wins by
\perfShadowGapAMD{} ms on the RX 9070 XT and loses by a factor of \perfShadowRatioNV{} on the
RTX 5070.
\end{itemize}

\section{Thesis structure}

Chapter~\ref{ch:pregled} reviews the theoretical background (rasterization versus ray tracing,
acceleration structures, the Vulkan ray-tracing extensions) and the existing real-time solutions the
evaluation compares against. Chapter~\ref{ch:implementacija} describes the implementation: the engine
and render graph, the acceleration-structure build, the four effects, the SVGF denoiser, the temporal
resolve, and the measurement tooling. Chapter~\ref{ch:analiza} isolates the cost of each effect and
measures reconstruction quality against a reference and against the existing solutions from
Chapter~\ref{ch:pregled}. Chapter~\ref{ch:zakljucak} states the limitations and outlines future work.
