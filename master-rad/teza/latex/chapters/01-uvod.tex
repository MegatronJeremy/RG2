This chapter states the problem the thesis addresses, why real-time realistic lighting is hard, and
how a hybrid ray-tracing approach fits an existing rasterizing engine. It closes with the research
question, the contributions, and a map of the remaining chapters.

\section{Problem i motivacija}

Real-time rendering is dominated by rasterization, which resolves primary visibility (what is
directly seen from the camera) very efficiently but has no native notion of what any surface point
can see beyond itself. Yet exactly that information is what the effects that make an image look real
depend on: indirect illumination bouncing between surfaces, contact shadows, glossy reflections, and
ambient occlusion all answer questions of the form ``what else is visible from here.'' Because
rasterization cannot answer those questions directly, real-time engines have long approximated them.
Screen-space methods (SSAO, screen-space reflections and global illumination) are cheap but see only
what is currently on screen, so they fail wherever the needed geometry is off-screen or newly
disoccluded \cite{mittring2007cryengine, bavoil2008hbao}. Precomputed methods bake lighting into
lightmaps or probes and cannot follow dynamic geometry. Voxel approaches such as voxel cone tracing
are scene-wide but coarse and prone to light leaking \cite{crassin2011vxgi}.

Hardware ray tracing changes what is affordable. Recent GPUs (NVIDIA RTX from 2018, AMD RDNA2 from
2020) added fixed-function units for bounding-volume-hierarchy traversal and ray-triangle
intersection, so casting a ray against the whole scene became fast enough to do per pixel at
interactive rates. This reopens the effects above as first-class, physically grounded passes that
sample the true scene rather than a screen-space proxy. The catch is that a full solution of the
rendering equation \cite{kajiya1986rendering} by path tracing remains far too expensive for
real-time at display resolution on this hardware, and any affordable ray budget (one or a few rays
per pixel) produces heavy noise. The practical path, and the one this thesis takes, is therefore
hybrid: rasterize primary visibility, add the secondary effects as ray-traced passes at low sample
counts, and reconstruct a clean image with a denoiser \cite{schied2017svgf}. Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} shows the
engine's hybrid output on the test scene.

\section{Za\v{s}to hibridni pristup i za\v{s}to ray query (a ne RT-pipeline)}

Vulkan offers two ways to trace rays. The ray-tracing pipeline introduces dedicated shader stages
(ray generation, closest-hit, miss, and others), a shader binding table, hardware scheduling of
rays across those stages, and recursion. Inline ray queries (\texttt{VK_KHR_ray_query}) instead let
any existing shader stage trace a ray inline: no separate pipeline, no shader binding table, no
recursion, with the caller writing the short traversal loop itself.

This work uses inline ray queries. The reason is architectural fit: the engine is a rasterizing
forward renderer, and inline queries let each effect drop in as an ordinary compute or fragment pass
without restructuring the renderer around a ray-tracing pipeline and its binding table. Effects can
be added one at a time, which matches a hybrid design where rays are a targeted addition on top of
rasterization rather than the primary architecture. The price is giving up hardware ray scheduling
across shader stages and recursion, but the effects here are shallow (a single bounce) and short, so
that price is small and is discussed again in Chapter~\ref{ch:zakljucak}.

\section{Cilj i istra\v{z}iva\v{c}ko pitanje}

The goal is to implement four ray-traced lighting effects, shadows, ambient occlusion, reflections,
and diffuse global illumination, through inline ray queries in a custom Vulkan engine, and to
characterize each one. The central research question has three parts: the per-effect cost on the frame
budget; how the ray count and the denoiser trade reconstruction quality against time; and how the
identical Vulkan implementation performs across GPU vendors (AMD RDNA4 versus NVIDIA Blackwell). Concretely
this breaks into measurable sub-questions: the per-effect GPU cost in milliseconds; the effect of
samples per pixel on quality (PSNR/SSIM) and on time; the quality and cost of the denoiser with it
enabled versus disabled; and the impact of tracing at half versus full resolution. The scope is
deliberately bounded: a single scene class, two current-generation GPUs from different vendors (an AMD Radeon RX 9070 XT, RDNA4, and an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 5070, Blackwell),
diffuse global illumination, and a converged path-traced reference for the quality metrics.

\section{Doprinosi}

The contributions of this thesis are: an integrated, openly licensed implementation of four hybrid
ray-query lighting effects inside one custom Vulkan engine; a per-effect GPU cost breakdown produced
by a reproducible A/B benchmark harness rather than ad-hoc captures; an evaluation of a
self-implemented SVGF denoiser shared across the shadow, ambient-occlusion, global-illumination, and
reflection signals, covering the quality it recovers, its cost, and its temporal stability; a reusable
half-resolution, G-buffer-prepass, denoise-and-upsample pattern shared across the effects; and a cross-vendor performance comparison of
the identical ray-query pipeline on AMD RDNA4 and NVIDIA Blackwell hardware, a fair same-code
comparison that is rarely reported.

\section{Struktura rada}

The remainder of the thesis follows the structure the assignment prescribes.
Chapter~\ref{ch:pregled} reviews the theoretical background (rasterization versus ray tracing,
acceleration structures, the Vulkan ray-tracing extensions) and the existing real-time solutions the
evaluation compares against. Chapter~\ref{ch:implementacija} describes the implementation: the engine
and render graph, the acceleration-structure build, the four effects, the SVGF denoiser, the temporal
resolve, and the measurement tooling. Chapter~\ref{ch:analiza} evaluates the system, isolating the
cost of each effect and measuring reconstruction quality against a reference and against the existing
solutions from Chapter~\ref{ch:pregled}. Chapter~\ref{ch:zakljucak} summarizes the results, states
the limitations, and outlines future work.
