\section{Rasterizacija naspram trasiranja zrakova}
\label{sec:rastervsrt}

Rasterization projects each triangle onto the image plane and resolves visibility with a depth
buffer: a stream operation that maps well to GPU hardware, although it processes one triangle at a
time with no access to the rest of the scene, so it cannot natively answer ``what else is visible
from this point.'' Ray tracing instead casts rays into the scene, finds their nearest intersections
\cite{moller1997raytri}, and estimates light transport by sampling \cite{pharr2016pbrt}, which
answers arbitrary point-to-point visibility (what indirect lighting, reflections, and occlusion
require) at the cost of per-ray traversal work and sampling noise. The common reference for both is
the rendering equation \cite{kajiya1986rendering}, splitting outgoing radiance $L_o$ at a point
$\mathbf{x}$ in direction $\omega_o$ into emitted radiance plus the reflected integral over the
hemisphere $\Omega$:
\begin{equation}
L_o(\mathbf{x}, \omega_o) = L_e(\mathbf{x}, \omega_o) + \int_{\Omega}
f_r(\mathbf{x}, \omega_i, \omega_o)\, L_i(\mathbf{x}, \omega_i)\, (\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n})\,
\mathrm{d}\omega_i,
\label{eq:rendering}
\end{equation}
where $f_r$ is the BRDF, $L_i$ the incident radiance, and $\mathbf{n}$ the surface normal.
Rasterization resolves the emitted and direct terms efficiently through projection, but the integral
needs incident radiance from every direction and therefore scene-wide visibility, which is exactly
what it cannot express and what ray tracing evaluates by sampling. A hybrid renderer uses each where
it is strong (Figure~\ref{fig:rastervsrt}) \cite{akenine2018rtr}.

\section{Strukture ubrzanja: BVH, TLAS/BLAS}
\label{sec:accel-pregled}

Testing every ray against every triangle is $O(\text{rays} \times \text{triangles})$ and hopeless at
scene scale, so ray tracing depends on a spatial acceleration structure. The standard one is a
bounding-volume hierarchy (BVH): a tree of nested bounding boxes whose traversal descends only into
boxes a ray actually enters, reducing the expected cost per ray to roughly logarithmic in the
triangle count. Build quality is governed by a cost heuristic, most commonly the surface area
heuristic (SAH), which prefers splits minimizing the probability-weighted surface area of the child
nodes and thus the expected number of tests per ray. On current GPUs the ray-box and ray-triangle
tests are fixed-function hardware, which is what makes a ray per pixel affordable in real time. How
much of the \emph{traversal} around those tests is also fixed-function differs by vendor, and the two
GPUs evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:analiza} sit on opposite sides of that line
(Figure~\ref{fig:traversal}): NVIDIA's RT cores walk the hierarchy in hardware \cite{nvidia_turing},
while on AMD's RDNA architectures the traversal loop is driven by shader instructions that issue
hardware intersection tests \cite{amd_rdna_isa}, so traversal competes with shading for the same
execution resources. That is a structural reason to \emph{expect} the same shader to cost differently
on the two vendors, not an attribution: Section~\ref{sec:cena} measures that the costs differ, and
this evaluation does not isolate how much of any measured gap comes from this mechanism rather than
from clocks, cache, or driver.

\section{Trasiranje zrakova u Vulkan-u: RT-pipeline naspram ray query}
\label{sec:vulkanrt}

Vulkan ray tracing rests on three extensions: \texttt{VK_KHR_acceleration_structure} to build and
manage the BVH, and two ways to trace against it, \texttt{VK_KHR_ray_tracing_pipeline} and
\texttt{VK_KHR_ray_query} \cite{khronos_rayquery}. The ray-tracing pipeline is the natural structure for a full path tracer:
hardware ray scheduling and recursion, at the price of dedicated stages (ray generation, closest-hit,
any-hit, miss, intersection) and a shader binding table mapping geometry to them. Inline ray queries
instead expose a \texttt{RayQuery} object usable from any
existing shader stage: the shader runs the traversal loop itself, with no binding table and no
recursion (Table~\ref{tab:pipeline-vs-rayquery}). This thesis uses ray queries because the effects
are bounded additions to an existing rasterizing renderer rather than a standalone tracer, as argued
in Section~\ref{sec:rendergraph}.

\section{Postoje\'ca real-time re\v{s}enja za GI i RT efekte}
\label{sec:postojeca}

The effects implemented here have a long line of real-time approximations
(Table~\ref{tab:postojeca}), in two tiers. The screen-space family is implemented in this engine and
measured head to head against the ray-traced path in Chapter~\ref{ch:analiza}, while the voxel,
probe, and reservoir methods are surveyed for positioning only, neither implemented nor measured here, so no
quantitative claim is made about them.

The tiers fail differently. Screen-space methods fail on \emph{missing information}: they see only
what is currently on screen, so an off-screen occluder or reflector is simply absent, which is the
class of error a ray query removes by consulting the real scene. Voxel and probe methods do see the
whole scene and fail instead on \emph{resolution}: their world representation is coarser than the
effect they reconstruct, which shows up as light leaking through thin walls or as smoothed-away
contact darkening. No amount of sampling fixes the first, but a finer grid, at a cost, mitigates the
second.

\section{Uklanjanje \v{s}uma (denoising) za RT}
\label{sec:denoise-pregled}

The integral in Equation~\eqref{eq:rendering} has no closed form, so it is estimated by Monte Carlo
integration: drawing $N$ directions $\omega_k$ from a probability density $p$ and averaging,
\begin{equation}
L_o \approx L_e + \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\frac{f_r(\omega_k)\, L_i(\omega_k)\, (\omega_k \cdot \mathbf{n})}{p(\omega_k)}.
\label{eq:mc}
\end{equation}
The estimator is unbiased, but its variance falls only as $1/N$, so the error (its standard
deviation) falls as $1/\sqrt{N}$: halving the noise costs four times the rays. At the one-to-few rays
per pixel a real-time budget allows, the raw estimate is heavily noisy, so denoising is a mandatory
part of the pipeline rather than an optional post-effect. This thesis implements Spatiotemporal
Variance-Guided Filtering (SVGF) \cite{schied2017svgf}, which combines temporal reprojection and
accumulation with a variance-guided \`a-trous spatial filter whose blur strength adapts to the
estimated per-pixel variance, so it smooths noisy regions while preserving converged detail. The main
alternatives are its adaptive successor A-SVGF \cite{schied2018asvgf}, NVIDIA's production Real-Time
Denoisers (ReBLUR and ReLAX) \cite{nvidia_nrd}, and machine-learning denoisers such as Intel's Open
Image Denoise \cite{intel_oidn}. Over the denoised result, temporal anti-aliasing accumulates jittered
frames to remove aliasing and to converge the remaining noise \cite{karis2014taa}. Its interaction
with the per-effect denoisers is part of the implementation in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementacija}.
